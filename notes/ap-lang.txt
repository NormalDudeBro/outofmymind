o Rhetorical Situation
-> Identify the specific audience the text addresses.
-> Infer its concerns from context, not stereotypes.
-> What beliefs, concerns, or shared values does it engage?
-> Name the occasion or problem prompting this argument.
-> State the change in judgment or action the author seeks.
-> Connect each choice to a need created by that situation.
-> Health-minded readers may still resist new regulation.
o Describe the Appeal
-> Explain how the quoted detail presents its claim.
-> Name the choice, then show how it works in these words.
-> Logos: identify the comparison or inference offered.
-> Ethos: identify the basis for trusting this judgment.
-> Pathos: identify the feeling or value the detail engages.
-> A choice can combine appeals; explain their interaction.
-> Do not substitute a device label for close analysis.
o Function - Author's Task
-> Function names the choice's job within the argument.
-> Ask why the author needs this move at this point.
-> Does it establish need, show feasibility, or answer a doubt?
-> Explain the gap this choice fills, not just its topic.
-> Evidence of harm establishes need, not a remedy's success.
-> A cost rebuttal can make a proposal seem less burdensome.
-> Tie that task to an audience need in this situation.
o Effect - Audience's Shift
-> Effect names a plausible change in audience thinking.
-> Start with a belief, value, feeling, or uncertainty.
-> Link one quoted detail to an inference readers could draw.
-> Explain why that inference might alter the starting view.
-> Trace: starting view -> detail -> inference -> shift.
-> Concrete risks may make a distant issue feel urgent.
-> Describe an invited response, not a guaranteed reaction.
o Justify - Purpose Connection
-> Explain why this particular shift serves the purpose.
-> Name the uncertainty, resistance, or motivation it addresses.
-> Show what readers can now more readily consider or accept.
-> Greater urgency may turn shared concern toward action.
-> That motivation supports a call for public-health reform.
-> Replace "strengthens the argument" with the actual link.
-> Ask: why does this shift matter for the desired response?
o Worked Connection
-> Hypothetical example, not a quotation from the article:
-> By comparing purchases before and after a warning policy,
-> a writer uses logical comparison to address doubts about
-> whether labels influence choices. A reported decline in
-> unhealthy purchases may suggest benefits to skeptics.
-> This shift makes adopting warnings easier to consider,
-> supporting the writer's effort to gain backing for reform.
o Framing and Diction
-> Identify what the wording makes central or peripheral.
-> Explain connotations through a specific word in the quote.
-> Ask whom the wording assigns agency or responsibility to.
-> Paraphrase: confusing packaging frames an information gap.
-> That framing makes clearer warnings a relevant response.
-> Readers may see help with choices, not just restriction.
-> Show why that interpretation favors the proposed policy.
o Evidence and Reasoning
-> Ask what doubt the author uses this evidence to address.
-> Explain how its scale, comparison, or source answers it.
-> A percentage shows proportion; a total makes scale tangible.
-> Moving between them can make a small change seem important.
-> Separate observed outcomes from projected benefits.
-> Do not attribute combined policy effects to labels alone.
-> Analyze the inference invited, not just the data supplied.
o Credibility and Emotion
-> Expertise builds trust only when relevant to the claim.
-> Explain why the cited source can judge this issue.
-> Transparency about limits can signal measured judgment.
-> Emotion needs a detail, not merely an emotional topic.
-> Show how a concrete risk engages a value such as health.
-> A concrete risk can make policy feel personally relevant.
-> Explain how trust or concern supports the next judgment.
o Objections and Qualifications
-> Identify the concern behind an objection: cost or freedom?
-> Ask whether the response refutes, reduces, or bypasses it.
-> Paraphrase: warnings inform choices rather than ban foods.
-> This distinction may ease fears of lost consumer freedom.
-> A limited claim asks readers to accept less, not nothing.
-> Modest benefits may seem credible without promising a cure.
-> Explain which objection the narrower claim avoids.
o Relationships and Sequence
-> Ask what an earlier choice prepares readers to accept.
-> Evidence of harm can make a remedy seem worth examining.
-> Precedent then addresses whether that remedy is workable.
-> A rebuttal can reduce resistance left by that evidence.
-> A qualification can keep the proposed benefit proportionate.
-> Explain the logical bridge, not just the order of devices.
-> Show how choices cooperate or create a revealing tension.
o Revision Checklist
-> Can I point to the quoted detail behind my analysis?
-> Does describe explain the appeal as it appears here?
-> Does function name the author's task in this situation?
-> Does effect link a starting view to a detail-based shift?
-> Does justify show why that shift advances the purpose?
-> Is the audience shift grounded in the text and situation?
-> Cut repeated claims; keep the reasons connecting each step.
