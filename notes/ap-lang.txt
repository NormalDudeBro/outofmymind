BLUE: 1. DESCRIBE - WHAT IS THE CHOICE?
Explain how the quoted evidence presents its information.
Do not simply repeat what the quotation says.
Identify what makes it logical, credible, or emotional.
Logos: what relationship supports the authors' conclusion?
Ethos: what gives readers a reason to trust their judgment?
Pathos: what specific feeling or value does it engage?
Notice how details are selected, compared, or emphasized.
Choose the feature you can explain most precisely.
Example: the authors support their proposal with reported
results from countries that have adopted labeling policies.
This grounds the proposal in precedent, not just prediction.
PURPLE: 2. FUNCTION - WHY USE IT HERE?
Identify the job this choice performs within the argument.
Think of function as the authors' argumentative task.
Are they establishing need, feasibility, or responsibility?
Are they answering an objection or limiting their claim?
Explain why that task matters in this rhetorical situation.
A policy proposal must address doubts about practical use.
Showing a problem exists does not show a solution will work.
Ask: what gap would remain without this evidence?
Example: the international results help answer whether
labeling can affect behavior, addressing a practical doubt
that could otherwise weaken the case for adopting it.
GREEN: 3. EFFECT - WHAT THINKING CAN CHANGE?
Think of effect as the audience's possible mental shift.
Name a relevant starting belief, concern, or uncertainty.
Infer it from the text, not stereotypes about the audience.
Explain the new interpretation the choice makes available.
Then connect that shift to a specific quoted detail.
Do not claim the audience automatically agrees.
Example: a skeptical reader may see labeling as untested.
The reference to implementation elsewhere offers precedent.
That detail gives the reader a reason to view the proposal
as less speculative, because it has moved beyond theory.
The connection is detail -> inference -> possible shift.
RED: 4. JUSTIFY - WHY DOES THAT SHIFT MATTER?
Connect the audience shift to the authors' overall purpose.
Name the barrier to agreement that the choice addresses.
Explain how reducing that barrier advances the larger claim.
Do not stop at "this strengthens the authors' argument."
Specify what readers are better positioned to accept.
Example: seeing labeling as workable removes one reason
to dismiss it, making U.S. adoption easier to consider.
This advances the authors' case for requiring warnings.
Do not claim precedent guarantees identical U.S. results.
Full chain: precedent -> answers feasibility doubts ->
less uncertainty -> greater openness to the policy.
BLACK: CONNECT THE STEPS IN PROSE
The four steps need not become four separate sentences.
Let each explanation create a reason for the next.
Example: By citing outcomes from countries with labeling
policies, the authors ground their proposal in precedent.
This addresses whether reform can affect consumption,
giving skeptical readers a reason to see it as workable
rather than purely theoretical. Reducing that uncertainty
helps the authors present U.S. adoption as worth pursuing.
Notice: each sentence develops rather than repeats.
ORANGE: CHECK THE CONNECTION BEFORE MOVING ON
Underline the detail your explanation actually depends on.
If you cannot locate it, your claim may exceed the evidence.
Circle vague words: effective, powerful, credible, emotional.
Replace them with the specific judgment being encouraged.
Check that function names a task, not an audience reaction.
Check that effect explains a reaction, not just the task.
Check that justify names the larger claim this serves.
Keep qualifications such as "may" where effects are uncertain.
Stop when the chain is complete; length is not depth.
BLUE: FIND THE MENTAL SHIFT
Ask what the choice makes readers see differently.
Not: "This makes the argument convincing."
Instead: explain why agreement now seems reasonable.
What assumption does the choice challenge or reinforce?
What doubt does it answer? What concern does it address?
Connect that change in thinking to the authors' purpose.
Describe invited responses, not guaranteed reactions.
PURPLE: EXPLAIN THE FRAMING
How the problem is defined shapes the proposed solution.
Confusing labels → consumers lack usable information.
That shifts attention from willpower to food packaging.
Clear warnings then seem helpful, not merely restrictive.
Ask: what becomes reasonable under this interpretation?
Examine whom the wording assigns responsibility to.
Explain the implications of words, not just their tone.
GREEN: EXPLAIN WHAT EVIDENCE PROVES
Do not stop at "statistics provide logos."
Name the specific uncertainty the evidence addresses.
Health data → establishes why the problem matters.
Foreign examples → show reform is not purely hypothetical.
Economic data → addresses fears about the cost of reform.
Ask what would remain unproven without this evidence.
Explain why that missing proof would limit agreement.
Distinguish observed results from predicted benefits.
ORANGE: CONNECT TO AUDIENCE RESISTANCE
Readers can favor health while resisting regulation.
Identify the value or concern behind their hesitation.
Information rather than bans → preserves consumer choice.
Cost rebuttals → make reform seem financially responsible.
Ask which reason for rejecting the proposal is addressed.
Does the response disprove, reduce, or bypass the concern?
Explain that move instead of merely calling it rebuttal.
Avoid assigning every reader the same beliefs.
RED: ANALYZE LIMITS AND TENSIONS
A limited claim can be easier to accept than a broad one.
"One step" framing avoids promising a complete solution.
Readers can support improvement without expecting a cure.
A distinction can prevent a damaging misunderstanding.
Processed versus ultraprocessed avoids blanket rejection.
Ask what overstatement or objection this prevents.
Explain how the qualification protects the main argument.
Do not stop at "the authors seem reasonable."
TEAL: CONNECT CHOICES TO EACH OTHER
Explain why a move occurs at this point in the argument.
Familiar shopping problems establish a recognizable need.
Research gives that need a basis beyond personal feeling.
Evidence of success moves from need toward feasibility.
Objections then address reasons to resist that solution.
Qualifications keep the proposed benefits proportionate.
Ask what an earlier move prepares readers to accept.
Explain cooperation, not just a list of separate devices.
