Page 1 · Building commentary · Lines 1–48

1TEAL: Rhetorical Situation
2Identify the specific audience the text addresses.
3Infer its concerns from context, not stereotypes.
4What beliefs, concerns, or shared values does it engage?
5Name the occasion or problem prompting this argument.
6State the change in judgment or action the author seeks.
7Connect each choice to a need created by that situation.
8Health-minded readers may still resist new regulation.
9BLUE: Describe the Appeal
10Explain how the quoted detail presents its claim.
11Name the choice, then show how it works in these words.
12Logos: identify the comparison or inference offered.
13Ethos: identify the basis for trusting this judgment.
14Pathos: identify the feeling or value the detail engages.
15A choice can combine appeals; explain their interaction.
16Do not substitute a device label for close analysis.
17PURPLE: Function - Author's Task
18Function names the choice's job within the argument.
19Ask why the author needs this move at this point.
20Does it establish need, show feasibility, or answer a doubt?
21Explain the gap this choice fills, not just its topic.
22Evidence of harm establishes need, not a remedy's success.
23A cost rebuttal can make a proposal seem less burdensome.
24Tie that task to an audience need in this situation.
25GREEN: Effect - Audience's Shift
26Effect names a plausible change in audience thinking.
27Start with a belief, value, feeling, or uncertainty.
28Link one quoted detail to an inference readers could draw.
29Explain why that inference might alter the starting view.
30Trace: starting view -> detail -> inference -> shift.
31Concrete risks may make a distant issue feel urgent.
32Describe an invited response, not a guaranteed reaction.
33RED: Justify - Purpose Connection
34Explain why this particular shift serves the purpose.
35Name the uncertainty, resistance, or motivation it addresses.
36Show what readers can now more readily consider or accept.
37Greater urgency may turn shared concern toward action.
38That motivation supports a call for public-health reform.
39Replace "strengthens the argument" with the actual link.
40Ask: why does this shift matter for the desired response?
41BLACK: Worked Connection
42Hypothetical example, not a quotation from the article:
43By comparing purchases before and after a warning policy,
44a writer uses logical comparison to address doubts about
45whether labels influence choices. A reported decline in
46unhealthy purchases may suggest benefits to skeptics.
47This shift makes adopting warnings easier to consider,
48supporting the writer's effort to gain backing for reform.

49PURPLE: Framing and Diction
50Identify what the wording makes central or peripheral.
51Explain connotations through a specific word in the quote.
52Ask whom the wording assigns agency or responsibility to.
53Paraphrase: confusing packaging frames an information gap.
54That framing makes clearer warnings a relevant response.
55Readers may see help with choices, not just restriction.
56Show why that interpretation favors the proposed policy.
57GREEN: Evidence and Reasoning
58Ask what doubt the author uses this evidence to address.
59Explain how its scale, comparison, or source answers it.
60A percentage shows proportion; a total makes scale tangible.
61Moving between them can make a small change seem important.
62Separate observed outcomes from projected benefits.
63Do not attribute combined policy effects to labels alone.
64Analyze the inference invited, not just the data supplied.
65BLUE: Credibility and Emotion
66Expertise builds trust only when relevant to the claim.
67Explain why the cited source can judge this issue.
68Transparency about limits can signal measured judgment.
69Emotion needs a detail, not merely an emotional topic.
70Show how a concrete risk engages a value such as health.
71A concrete risk can make policy feel personally relevant.
72Explain how trust or concern supports the next judgment.
73ORANGE: Objections and Qualifications
74Identify the concern behind an objection: cost or freedom?
75Ask whether the response refutes, reduces, or bypasses it.
76Paraphrase: warnings inform choices rather than ban foods.
77This distinction may ease fears of lost consumer freedom.
78A limited claim asks readers to accept less, not nothing.
79Modest benefits may seem credible without promising a cure.
80Explain which objection the narrower claim avoids.
81TEAL: Relationships and Sequence
82Ask what an earlier choice prepares readers to accept.
83Evidence of harm can make a remedy seem worth examining.
84Precedent then addresses whether that remedy is workable.
85A rebuttal can reduce resistance left by that evidence.
86A qualification can keep the proposed benefit proportionate.
87Explain the logical bridge, not just the order of devices.
88Show how choices cooperate or create a revealing tension.
89BLACK: Revision Checklist
90Can I point to the quoted detail behind my analysis?
91Does describe explain the appeal as it appears here?
92Does function name the author's task in this situation?
93Does effect link a starting view to a detail-based shift?
94Does justify show why that shift advances the purpose?
95Is the audience shift grounded in the text and situation?
96Cut repeated claims; keep the reasons connecting each step.
