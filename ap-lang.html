Page 1 · Building commentary · Lines 1–24

1o Rhetorical Situation
2-> Identify the specific audience the text addresses.
3-> Infer its concerns from context, not stereotypes.
4-> What beliefs, concerns, or shared values does it engage?
5-> Name the occasion or problem prompting this argument.
6-> State the change in judgment or action the author seeks.
7-> Connect each choice to a need created by that situation.
8-> Health-minded readers may still resist new regulation.
9o Describe the Appeal
10-> Explain how the quoted detail presents its claim.
11-> Name the choice, then show how it works in these words.
12-> Logos: identify the comparison or inference offered.
13-> Ethos: identify the basis for trusting this judgment.
14-> Pathos: identify the feeling or value the detail engages.
15-> A choice can combine appeals; explain their interaction.
16-> Do not substitute a device label for close analysis.
17o Function - Author's Task
18-> Function names the choice's job within the argument.
19-> Ask why the author needs this move at this point.
20-> Does it establish need, show feasibility, or answer a doubt?
21-> Explain the gap this choice fills, not just its topic.
22-> Evidence of harm establishes need, not a remedy's success.
23-> A cost rebuttal can make a proposal seem less burdensome.
24-> Tie that task to an audience need in this situation.

Page 2 · Building commentary · Lines 25–48

25o Effect - Audience's Shift
26-> Effect names a plausible change in audience thinking.
27-> Start with a belief, value, feeling, or uncertainty.
28-> Link one quoted detail to an inference readers could draw.
29-> Explain why that inference might alter the starting view.
30-> Trace: starting view -> detail -> inference -> shift.
31-> Concrete risks may make a distant issue feel urgent.
32-> Describe an invited response, not a guaranteed reaction.
33o Justify - Purpose Connection
34-> Explain why this particular shift serves the purpose.
35-> Name the uncertainty, resistance, or motivation it addresses.
36-> Show what readers can now more readily consider or accept.
37-> Greater urgency may turn shared concern toward action.
38-> That motivation supports a call for public-health reform.
39-> Replace "strengthens the argument" with the actual link.
40-> Ask: why does this shift matter for the desired response?
41o Worked Connection
42-> Hypothetical example, not a quotation from the article:
43-> By comparing purchases before and after a warning policy,
44-> a writer uses logical comparison to address doubts about
45-> whether labels influence choices. A reported decline in
46-> unhealthy purchases may suggest benefits to skeptics.
47-> This shift makes adopting warnings easier to consider,
48-> supporting the writer's effort to gain backing for reform.

Page 3 · Deepening analysis · Lines 49–72

49o Framing and Diction
50-> Identify what the wording makes central or peripheral.
51-> Explain connotations through a specific word in the quote.
52-> Ask whom the wording assigns agency or responsibility to.
53-> Paraphrase: confusing packaging frames an information gap.
54-> That framing makes clearer warnings a relevant response.
55-> Readers may see help with choices, not just restriction.
56-> Show why that interpretation favors the proposed policy.
57o Evidence and Reasoning
58-> Ask what doubt the author uses this evidence to address.
59-> Explain how its scale, comparison, or source answers it.
60-> A percentage shows proportion; a total makes scale tangible.
61-> Moving between them can make a small change seem important.
62-> Separate observed outcomes from projected benefits.
63-> Do not attribute combined policy effects to labels alone.
64-> Analyze the inference invited, not just the data supplied.
65o Credibility and Emotion
66-> Expertise builds trust only when relevant to the claim.
67-> Explain why the cited source can judge this issue.
68-> Transparency about limits can signal measured judgment.
69-> Emotion needs a detail, not merely an emotional topic.
70-> Show how a concrete risk engages a value such as health.
71-> A concrete risk can make policy feel personally relevant.
72-> Explain how trust or concern supports the next judgment.

Page 4 · Deepening analysis · Lines 73–96

73o Objections and Qualifications
74-> Identify the concern behind an objection: cost or freedom?
75-> Ask whether the response refutes, reduces, or bypasses it.
76-> Paraphrase: warnings inform choices rather than ban foods.
77-> This distinction may ease fears of lost consumer freedom.
78-> A limited claim asks readers to accept less, not nothing.
79-> Modest benefits may seem credible without promising a cure.
80-> Explain which objection the narrower claim avoids.
81o Relationships and Sequence
82-> Ask what an earlier choice prepares readers to accept.
83-> Evidence of harm can make a remedy seem worth examining.
84-> Precedent then addresses whether that remedy is workable.
85-> A rebuttal can reduce resistance left by that evidence.
86-> A qualification can keep the proposed benefit proportionate.
87-> Explain the logical bridge, not just the order of devices.
88-> Show how choices cooperate or create a revealing tension.
89o Revision Checklist
90-> Can I point to the quoted detail behind my analysis?
91-> Does describe explain the appeal as it appears here?
92-> Does function name the author's task in this situation?
93-> Does effect link a starting view to a detail-based shift?
94-> Does justify show why that shift advances the purpose?
95-> Is the audience shift grounded in the text and situation?
96-> Cut repeated claims; keep the reasons connecting each step.
