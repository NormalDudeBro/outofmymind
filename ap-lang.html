1BLUE: 1. DESCRIBE - WHAT IS THE CHOICE?
2Explain how the quoted evidence presents its information.
3Do not simply repeat what the quotation says.
4Identify what makes it logical, credible, or emotional.
5Logos: what relationship supports the authors' conclusion?
6Ethos: what gives readers a reason to trust their judgment?
7Pathos: what specific feeling or value does it engage?
8Notice how details are selected, compared, or emphasized.
9Choose the feature you can explain most precisely.
10Example: the authors support their proposal with reported
11results from countries that have adopted labeling policies.
12This grounds the proposal in precedent, not just prediction.
13PURPLE: 2. FUNCTION - WHY USE IT HERE?
14Identify the job this choice performs within the argument.
15Think of function as the authors' argumentative task.
16Are they establishing need, feasibility, or responsibility?
17Are they answering an objection or limiting their claim?
18Explain why that task matters in this rhetorical situation.
19A policy proposal must address doubts about practical use.
20Showing a problem exists does not show a solution will work.
21Ask: what gap would remain without this evidence?
22Example: the international results help answer whether
23labeling can affect behavior, addressing a practical doubt
24that could otherwise weaken the case for adopting it.
25GREEN: 3. EFFECT - WHAT THINKING CAN CHANGE?
26Think of effect as the audience's possible mental shift.
27Name a relevant starting belief, concern, or uncertainty.
28Infer it from the text, not stereotypes about the audience.
29Explain the new interpretation the choice makes available.
30Then connect that shift to a specific quoted detail.
31Do not claim the audience automatically agrees.
32Example: a skeptical reader may see labeling as untested.

33The reference to implementation elsewhere offers precedent.
34That detail gives the reader a reason to view the proposal
35as less speculative, because it has moved beyond theory.
36The connection is detail -> inference -> possible shift.
37RED: 4. JUSTIFY - WHY DOES THAT SHIFT MATTER?
38Connect the audience shift to the authors' overall purpose.
39Name the barrier to agreement that the choice addresses.
40Explain how reducing that barrier advances the larger claim.
41Do not stop at "this strengthens the authors' argument."
42Specify what readers are better positioned to accept.
43Example: seeing labeling as workable removes one reason
44to dismiss it, making U.S. adoption easier to consider.
45This advances the authors' case for requiring warnings.
46Do not claim precedent guarantees identical U.S. results.
47Full chain: precedent -> answers feasibility doubts ->
48less uncertainty -> greater openness to the policy.
49BLACK: CONNECT THE STEPS IN PROSE
50The four steps need not become four separate sentences.
51Let each explanation create a reason for the next.
52Example: By citing outcomes from countries with labeling
53policies, the authors ground their proposal in precedent.
54This addresses whether reform can affect consumption,
55giving skeptical readers a reason to see it as workable
56rather than purely theoretical. Reducing that uncertainty
57helps the authors present U.S. adoption as worth pursuing.
58Notice: each sentence develops rather than repeats.
59ORANGE: CHECK THE CONNECTION BEFORE MOVING ON
60Underline the detail your explanation actually depends on.
61If you cannot locate it, your claim may exceed the evidence.
62Circle vague words: effective, powerful, credible, emotional.
63Replace them with the specific judgment being encouraged.
64Check that function names a task, not an audience reaction.

65Check that effect explains a reaction, not just the task.

67Keep qualifications such as "may" where effects are uncertain.

69BLUE: FIND THE MENTAL SHIFT
70Ask what the choice makes readers see differently.
71Not: "This makes the argument convincing."
72Instead: explain why agreement now seems reasonable.
73What assumption does the choice challenge or reinforce?
74What doubt does it answer? What concern does it address?
75Connect that change in thinking to the authors' purpose.
76Describe invited responses, not guaranteed reactions.
77PURPLE: EXPLAIN THE FRAMING
78How the problem is defined shapes the proposed solution.
79Confusing labels → consumers lack usable information.
80That shifts attention from willpower to food packaging.
81Clear warnings then seem helpful, not merely restrictive.
82Ask: what becomes reasonable under this interpretation?
83Examine whom the wording assigns responsibility to.
84Explain the implications of words, not just their tone.

103RED: ANALYZE LIMITS AND TENSIONS
104A limited claim can be easier to accept than a broad one.
105"One step" framing avoids promising a complete solution.
106Readers can support improvement without expecting a cure.
107A distinction can prevent a damaging misunderstanding.
108Processed versus ultraprocessed avoids blanket rejection.
109Ask what overstatement or objection this prevents.
110Explain how the qualification protects the main argument.
111Do not stop at "the authors seem reasonable."
112TEAL: CONNECT CHOICES TO EACH OTHER
113Explain why a move occurs at this point in the argument.
114Familiar shopping problems establish a recognizable need.
115Research gives that need a basis beyond personal feeling.
116Evidence of success moves from need toward feasibility.
117Objections then address reasons to resist that solution.
118Qualifications keep the proposed benefits proportionate.
119Ask what an earlier move prepares readers to accept.
