Of Mice and Men

by John Steinbeck

Of Mice and Men is John Steinbeck's 1937 novella about George Milton and Lennie Small, two migrant ranch workers travelling through California's Salinas Valley who share one dream: a small place of their own, with rabbits to tend.

Author Information

What the handout asks: "Include important facts of the author's life like the the 5Ws (who, what, when, where, & why)." (Active Reading Pamphlet.pdf, p. 1)[6] The five answers below use the Nobel Prize biography and the cited account of the novella's development; keep the source distinction in mind.

John Steinbeck

Who: John Steinbeck, American novelist and short-story writer
When: born 1902; died December 20, 1968
Where: born in Salinas, California; died in New York City
What: Tortilla Flat (1935), In Dubious Battle (1936), Of Mice and Men (1937), The Long Valley (1938), The Grapes of Wrath (1939); Nobel Prize in Literature, 1962
Why: his books treat the economic lives of rural labourers, and the Nobel Committee honoured them for "sympathetic humour and keen social perception"[3][2]

Steinbeck "came from a family of moderate means" in Salinas and "worked his way through college at Stanford University but never graduated." In 1925 he went to New York, where he tried for a few years to establish himself as a freelance writer, failed, and returned to California. After publishing some novels and short stories, he first became widely known with Tortilla Flat (1935).[2]

He knew the world of this novella first-hand, which is the real answer to the handout's why. Steinbeck based the novella on his own experiences as a teenager working alongside migrant farm workers in the 1910s, before the arrival of the Okies he would later dramatise in The Grapes of Wrath, and he told The New York Times in 1937:[4]

"I was a bindlestiff myself for quite a spell. I worked in the same country that the story is laid in."[4]

The Nobel biography places Of Mice and Men (1937) between In Dubious Battle (1936) and The Long Valley (1938), with The Grapes of Wrath following in 1939, and describes his novels as social novels "dealing with the economic problems of rural labour."[2] That is the link between the man and the book: the economics are not decoration in this novella, they are the engine of the plot.

"for his realistic and imaginative writings, combining as they do sympathetic humour and keen social perception"[3]

That was the wording of the 1962 Nobel Prize in Literature. Steinbeck died in New York City in 1968.[2]

For your pamphlet: copy the 5Ws box, then add one sentence of your own on why this author matters to this book. Be careful with dates: the Nobel biography gives Steinbeck's birth year only, so if you want a full birth date, write the source you took it from rather than borrowing it from here.

Contextual Information

What the handout asks: "Explain how the genre, history or culture of when the book was written is reflected in the book." (Active Reading Pamphlet.pdf, p. 1)[6] It asks for one of the three — genre, or history, or culture — so you only need the one you can support best. All three are visible in Of Mice and Men, and all three are set out below so you can choose.

Genre

Steinbeck wrote a novella built to be played. It has six chapters arranged as three acts of two, roughly 30,000 words, and reads like a script: scenes happen in real time, in few locations, mostly through dialogue.[4] One critic called the form a "play-novelette," and Steinbeck's stated aim was a novel that could be played from its lines or a play that could be read like a novel.[4] Within months it was staged as written by the Theater Union of San Francisco, opening May 21, 1937 and running about two months; Steinbeck's adapted version then opened on Broadway on November 23, 1937, produced by Sam H. Harris, directed by George S. Kaufman, and ran 207 performances; a film followed in 1939.[4] That dramatic shape is why the book's emotion lands almost entirely through spoken lines rather than narration — and it is a clean, checkable answer for the genre box.

History

The story is set during the Great Depression, among itinerant ranch workers in California who move from job to job and live out of bedrolls, "searching for jobs during the Great Depression."[4] Steinbeck drew on his own teenage years working alongside migrant farm workers before the Dust Bowl migration he later dramatised in The Grapes of Wrath.[4] The economics are the engine of the plot: steady work is scarce, a man can be fired without notice, and buying even a few acres is far beyond a labourer's wages. The novella came out in 1937, in the middle of the Depression, and was chosen as a Book of the Month Club selection before it was published — the largest positive response to any of Steinbeck's work to that date.[4]

Culture

  • A segregated West: Crooks sleeps apart from the other men in the harness room and is kept out of the bunkhouse; the threat used against him shows what a white accuser could set in motion.
  • Women at the margins: Curley's wife is never given a name and is spoken about before she is allowed to speak, which the story itself finally corrects in the barn.
  • Disability before the language existed: Lennie's condition is described with the harsh period words of 1937; modern editions and classrooms read the same behaviour with different vocabulary.
  • A contested classroom text: the novella has been banned or challenged repeatedly for vulgarity, profanity, and racial language, and appears on the American Library Association's most-challenged lists.[4]
"The best laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley"[7]

The title comes from Robert Burns's 1785 poem "To a Mouse," in which a ploughman apologises to a mouse whose nest he has destroyed. Steinbeck first called the story Something That Happened, then took the title from the poem after reading it.[4] The culture of the time also shaped the words: the poem's idea that careful plans still come to grief matches a decade in which honest work did not guarantee food.

For your pamphlet: you only need one of the three. A short paragraph can say: Steinbeck wrote a play-shaped novella (genre) during the Great Depression, from his own years among California migrant workers (history), in a segregated, tightly gendered West whose language and censorship we still argue about (culture).

Important Characters

A character worth putting on the pamphlet can be described in all four.[6]

George Milton

Lennie's companion • Small, quick, sharp-featured

A small, restless man who looks after Lennie and keeps the shared dream alive. He complains about Lennie and never leaves him; without Lennie he would be just another lonely worker, earning a stake only to spend it in town every Saturday night.

Mental

Pragmatic: he sizes up Curley in a minute, coaches Lennie on what to say to the boss, and plans the farm in detail.

Moral

Loyal: after the Weed incident he stays with Lennie instead of abandoning him, and carries the burden of protecting him.

Physical

Slender and "small and quick," with dark features and restless eyes — Lennie's opposite in every measurement.

Social

Guarded: he snaps at Lennie in front of strangers to look tough, yet is the only man on the ranch with a true friend.

Key scene: George tells Slim the whole story of Weed, then admits he could live easily without Lennie — and would not trade him for it. Ch. 3 • search: "this girl in a red dress"

Lennie Small

George's companion • Huge, powerful, childlike

A very large man with a severe intellectual disability, remembered by George since childhood. He loves soft things and forgets everything except the dream of tending rabbits. His strength, uncontrolled, drives the tragedy.

Mental

Forgetful: he cannot hold instructions, but he can repeat the farm dream word for word because he wants it more than anything.

Moral

Kind: he means no harm to any living thing, and his worst acts all begin as affection.

Physical

Enormous and bear-like, dragging his feet as he walks, with a strength he cannot judge.

Social

Dependent: he needs George for words, rules, and safety, and is frightened to his core of being left alone.

Key scene: Lennie asks George to tell the story again, promising this time he will remember. Ch. 1 • search: "I remember about the rabbits"

Candy

Old swamper • One hand, loyal old dog

An aging ranch handyman who sweeps the bunkhouse and fears being thrown out when he can no longer work. His dog's death breaks something in him, and he throws his life savings into George and Lennie's farm dream.

Mental

Calculating: once he hears the farm described, he works out the exact money needed and offers to join.

Moral

Regretful: he blames himself for letting a stranger shoot his dog, and carries it afterward.

Physical

Ailing: an old man missing a hand, aware that his body is running out of uses.

Social

Wistful: he wants a place at the fire, a corner of the farm, and someone to grow old beside.

Key scene: Carlson shoots Candy's dog; Candy lies still, then offers George his money for the little farm. Ch. 3 • search: "I ought to of shot that dog myself"

Crooks

Stable buck • Lives alone in the harness room

The Black stable hand, isolated by the racism of the ranch. Proud and bitter, he reads books in his cramped room. Lennie's ignorance of the rules lets Crooks say out loud what he really wants — and then take it back.

Mental

Skeptical: he has seen a lifetime of men with impossible plans and expects this one to fail too.

Moral

Proud: he guards his dignity fiercely, and is ashamed later of how he frightened Lennie.

Physical

Crooked-backed: a permanent injury that both names him and keeps him to heavy stable work.

Social

Withdrawn: barred from the bunkhouse, he keeps the other men out before they can keep him out.

Key scene: Crooks lets slip how lonely he is, then asks whether he might hoe a little garden on the men's farm — and laughs it off. Ch. 4 • search: "A guy needs somebody to be near him"

Curley's Wife

The boss's son's wife • Never named

A young, restless woman who wanders the ranch looking for someone to talk to. Everyone warns the men away from her, but in the barn she finally says what she wanted from life: to be seen, and to be somebody.

Mental

Naïve: she still believes a stranger's promise of Hollywood wrote to her and simply never came.

Moral

Cruel when cornered: she threatens Crooks with a lynching to re-establish her power in the room.

Physical

Pretty: curling iron, red nails, soft hair — the appearance she staked her hopes on.

Social

Isolated: she is married, but her husband forbids conversation, so she flirts because it is the only speech available.

Key scene: In the barn she admits she is never allowed to talk to anyone, then tells Lennie about the movies she almost was in. Ch. 5 • search: "I can't talk to nobody but Curley"

Slim

Jerkline skinner • The authority of the ranch

The lead driver of the mule team, respected by everyone, including Curley. He sees straight through people: he is the only character who understands what George and Lennie are to each other, and the only one who understands what George does at the end.

Mental

Insightful: he reads character instantly, and reads the final scene correctly without being told.

Moral

Decisive: he drowns the weakest puppies so the litter can live, and stands by George afterward.

Physical

Steady: tall, quiet-handed, with a deep voice and a natural calm that settles rooms.

Social

Respected: the men take his word as final, and he speaks rarely enough that it carries.

Key scene: Slim walks George away from the river and tells him he had no choice. Ch. 6 • search: "You hadda, George"

Curley

The boss's son • Ex-boxer, combative

The boss's small, aggressive son, recently a boxer, wearing a glove full of vaseline to keep his left hand soft for his wife. He picks fights with large men and attacks Lennie twice; the second fight costs him his hand.

Mental

Defensive: he interprets laughter, size, and friendliness as threats to his status.

Moral

Selfish: he treats the ranch hands as rivals, and his wife as property he must police.

Physical

Compact and quick, a trained fighter who depends on speed and resentment.

Social

Abrasive: his father's position lets him wound people without ever paying for it.

Key scene: Curley attacks Lennie in the bunkhouse and Lennie holds his hand until George tells him to stop. Ch. 3 • search: "glove's fulla vaseline" (Ch. 2 introduces him)

Carlson

Ranch hand • Strong, tactless

A thick-bodied hand who cannot stand the smell of Candy's old dog and shoots it without sentiment. He is also the man who finds Curley's wife's body and, at the very end, cannot understand George and Slim's grief.

Mental

Blunt: he says exactly what he notices and never imagines that others feel differently.

Moral

Indifferent: he offers to shoot another man's dog as a favour, with no grasp of what it means.

Physical

Robust* — "strong and healthy; hardy; vigorous" — strong enough to lift the dog easily and confident with a gun.

Social

Boorish* — "unmannered; crude; insensitive" — his last question, what is wrong with those two, shows he will never understand the story he just watched.

Key scene: Carlson takes Candy's dog out to shoot it, and Candy does not stop him. Ch. 3 • search: "I ought to of shot that dog myself"

"Guys like us, that work on ranches, are the loneliest guys in the world."[1]

Read Curley's wife against Steinbeck's own account of her, which is useful for the group argument but should not be treated as the only meaning: he described her as "not a person, she's a symbol. She has no function, except to be a foil – and a danger to Lennie," and in a later letter to the actress who played her, he wrote that her harsh, flirtatious behaviour is "a method of defense against her fear for isolation and desperate craving for companionship."[4] Both readings — symbol first, then person — are supported; the novella itself finally chooses the second.

Unforgettable Moments

What the handout asks: "Answer with descriptive phrases; include page numbers" for the five stages — Exposition, Rising Action, Climax, Falling Action, Resolution (Active Reading Pamphlet.pdf, p. 1).[6] Write in phrases, not summaries: the box under each stage is a phrase you can copy into the pamphlet once you have checked it against your own page.
Reading locators: use the chapter as the anchor and the search phrase to find the scene in your copy. Record the page from your copy before submitting; no physical edition was consulted for pagination.[5]

Exposition

A hot evening on the Salinas River, a few miles south of Soledad: two men in denim come down the path in single file, one small and quick, one huge and dragging his feet like a bear. They drink, squabble, and reveal what they are running from — the incident in Weed — and George rehearses the dream Lennie lives for: a little place, cows, pigs, and rabbits.[1]

Ch. 1 • search: "A few miles south of Soledad"

Rising Action

At the ranch the next day: the boss questions them, Curley takes an instant dislike to Lennie, and George warns Lennie away from the boss's son's flirtatious wife. George and Slim talk, and George retells Weed — Lennie held a girl's red dress and would not let go. Candy's old dog is put down by Carlson, and Candy, gutted, offers his life savings toward the farm. Curley attacks Lennie and his hand is crushed. In the stable, Crooks tests the dream and, for a moment, wants in. Curley's wife tells Lennie her Hollywood dream. The farm suddenly feels close enough to buy.[1]

Ch. 2–4 • search: "that glove's fulla vaseline" • "I ought to of shot that dog myself" • "A guy needs somebody to be near him"

Climax

In the barn, Lennie has accidentally killed his puppy. Curley's wife finds him, confesses her loneliness, and lets him stroke her hair; when he does not stop, she panics and screams, and Lennie, frightened, breaks her neck. He covers her with hay and slips away to the river, to the hiding place they agreed on.[1]

Ch. 5 • search: "Please don't do that. George'll be mad"

Falling Action

Candy finds the body and knows the farm is dead; Curley organises a posse to hunt Lennie down; George slips away first. At the pool, Lennie's fear erupts into a hallucination — Aunt Clara and a giant rabbit accuse him of ruining everything — until George arrives and calms him by beginning the dream again.[1]

Ch. 5–6 • search: "Aunt Clara"

Resolution

With the men shouting in the distance, George tells the farm story once more while Lennie looks across the river, then shoots him from behind. Slim understands immediately and leads George away: "You hadda, George. I swear you hadda." Curley and Carlson are left staring after them, unable to read what they have just seen.[1]

Ch. 6 • search: "Look acrost the river, Lennie"

"You hadda, George. I swear you hadda."[1]
Central conflict: tenderness in a world with no safety net. Two men build a future out of a promise; an accident, a frightened scream, and a boss's angry son take it apart. The plot's frame — the same pool at the first and last evening — tells you before you begin that the story will close a circle. Note that the summary of events above follows the novella's own account of what happens; the why of the shooting is argued openly in Theme, and different readers defend it differently.

Setting

What the handout asks: "Setting — The background against which action takes place," built from four elements: "(1) the geographical location…; (2) the occupations and daily manner of living of the characters; (3) the time period…; and (4) the general environment of the characters" (Setting Details.pdf, p. 1).[6] Then "gather descriptions using 1 of 4 setting details that show not just tell."[6]
The four lenses: before you choose, the handout asks whether each setting "influence[s] the plot, setting (imagery), character, or function[s] as a symbol," and whether it "reflect[s] or contrast[s] the characters' experience" (Setting Details.pdf, p. 1). All four lenses are worked through below.

Geographical Location

The action sits in California's Salinas Valley: a green pool on the Salinas River a few miles south of Soledad, with the Gabilan Mountains on one side and the foothills on the other; then a ranch of bunkhouse, barn, harness room, and whitewashed walls; and finally the river pool again. The windows and doors matter — the bunkhouse's solid door, Crooks's square four-paned window, the barn's sliding entrance — because they decide who may enter where.[1]

Ch. 1 • search: "A few miles south of Soledad"

Occupations and Daily Living

Itinerant ranch hands: bucking grain bags, feeding mules and chickens, cleaning stalls, branding, and waiting on the boss's orders. Wages are drawn and spent; Saturdays mean town. Candy sweeps, Crooks tends the horses, Slim drives the mule team, Curley shadows his father's property.

Time Period

Late-1930s California, during the Great Depression, in harvest season — thrashing machines are running, the days are hot, and the shade climbs the hills by evening. The story spans only a few days, from one evening at the pool to the next visit there after the ranch events.[1]

General Environment

The social and emotional weather: loneliness as the default condition, money fear under every decision, a colour line drawn through the harness room, a woman with nowhere to stand, and a hierarchy that runs boss → son → hands. Guides have noted that the town's name, Soledad, is Spanish for "solitude."[4]

"A few miles south of Soledad, the Salinas River dropped in close to the hillside"[1]

Descriptions That Show, Not Tell

Seen
Water "slipped twinkling over the yellow sands"; crisp sycamore leaves lying deep; rabbits sitting "as quietly as little gray sculptured stones"; evening shade climbing the hills; the whitewashed bunkhouse with eight bunks; the barn stacked to the roof with new hay.
Heard
Footsteps on crisp leaves; a heron beating up river; Lennie snorting at the pool "like a horse"; horseshoes ringing outside the bunkhouse; the posse shouting on the last evening.
Felt
Warm river water; the grit of sand; the hot day cooling into evening; Crooks's liniment; the softness of hair that the story keeps returning to.

Ch. 1 • search: "A few miles south of Soledad"

The Four Lenses

  • Plot: The pool is the frame — the men sleep there before the ranch and meet there after it, and the agreed hiding place makes the final meeting inevitable.
  • Setting (imagery): Heat, shade, water, and hay carry the mood; the same river that opens in evening calm closes in evening calm.
  • Character: Crooks's shed "leaned off the wall of the barn" — his isolation is built into the floor plan. Curley's wife is forever "in the barn" or "in the doorway," a person with no allowed room.
  • Symbol: The little farm with the cow and rabbits stands for security itself; the pool is a birthplace and a killing ground at once.
Scene traits to choose from: the handout's list supplies the word choices, and its starred words are on the year-end vocabulary test (Setting Details.pdf, pp. 1–2).[6] For the pool scenes: serene, hushed, tranquil; for the bunkhouse and barn: cramped, oppressive, crowded; for the last evening: foreboding*, ominous*, somber*, sinister*; for Crooks's room and the women's doorway: isolated, lonely, bleak, desolate*.

Narrator

What the handout asks: "Identify the narrator(s) and gather evidence of how the perspective influences the story using at least 2 of the bolded words on your active reading pamphlet" (Narration Traits.pdf, p. 1).[6] Five of those bolded terms are used below: Third Person, Omniscient, Limited Third Person, Perspective, Bias, and Narrative Distance.

Of Mice and Men has no named storyteller. An unseen narrator stands outside the story and reports it in the third person — the "(s)he" form, defined in the handout as when "the narrator is looking in on the person or people being talked about" — in past tense, moving like a camera from scene to scene.[6]

Third Person

The narrator is looking in on the people being talked about. Everything is reported: two men come down the path to the river; Lennie fills his whole head in the water; George raises the gun and his hand shakes. No "I" ever appears, so no character is telling you this — which means you must judge every action yourself.

Evidence • Ch. 1 • search: "A few miles south of Soledad"

Selective Omniscient Narration

The narrator is not strictly limited to one character: stage directions reveal external actions, and occasional narration gives brief access to a character's sensations or thoughts. But the text does not establish unrestricted knowledge of every mind. Lennie's Aunt Clara episode, for example, is explicitly presented as his hallucination rather than proof that the narrator is all-knowing. A careful description is third-person narration with selective shifts in closeness, not simply "omniscient."

Evidence • Ch. 6 • search: "Aunt Clara"

Narrative Distance

The camera stays closest to George and Lennie, especially in Chapter One, where the dream is learned through their bickering. Other characters are initially filtered through what the men say about them: Curley's wife is warned about for chapters before the reader hears her at length in the barn. That distance can make the reader adopt the ranch's suspicion before her account complicates it. This is an effect of the narration; it does not prove a single authorial intention.

Evidence • Ch. 2 • search: "I'm lookin' for Curley" (first time she speaks)

Perspective

How the characters view and process what is happening, as opposed to who narrates it: the same farm dream looks different through each pair of eyes. To Lennie it is rabbits; to George it is freedom from needing anyone's permission; to Crooks it is a trick that has never once paid off; to Candy it is a way not to be discarded. The narrator reports all four views without endorsing one.

"A guy needs somebody to be near him."[1]
Bias and distance: The narrator's restraint can be read as neutrality, but the choice to leave Curley's wife unnamed and to filter her early appearances through the men's warnings also shapes the reader's perspective. Whether that amounts to narrator bias is arguable. Bias, in the handout's sense, is "prejudice in favor of or against one thing, person, or group compared with another"; narrative distance is "the distance between the narrator and other elements of the fictional world," and it ranges from small to large.[6] At the end, the shot is reported plainly, without an explicit moral verdict; readers must interpret the surrounding actions and silence.
How the perspective influences the story: because the narration usually stays outside the characters, moments of unusually close access — Crooks's loneliness, the wife's account, Lennie's hallucination — stand out. Its restraint helps the ending hold competing readings: the narrator supplies actions and consequences but leaves readers to judge George's choice.

Attitude & Emotional Sparks

What the handout asks: two different fields. Attitude: "Select 1 emotional scene and provide evidence from the story, describing it using an attitude word." Emotional Sparks: "Include excerpts of quotes and page number(s) and explain your emotional reaction using mood words." (Active Reading Pamphlet.pdf, p. 2)[6]
Keep the two apart: Attitude/Tone is "the attitude a writer takes toward the subject of a work, the characters in it, or the audience" — the handout's key is Attitude = Author. Mood "evokes feelings in readers through words and descriptions; it creates an emotional situation" — the handout's key is Mood = Me. (Attitude vs. Mood.pdf, p. 1)[6] When you choose, the handout says, "start in the center with the overarching emotion, but then go out into the fan to make the best word choice for the tone."

Attitude: the Emotional Scene

Selected scene: the last evening at the pool (Ch. 6), when George shoots Lennie while telling him the farm dream one more time. Ch. 6 • search: "Look acrost the river, Lennie"

Evidence from the story: George does not shout. He answers Lennie's worry ("I ain't mad") and begins the whole catalogue of the farm — the cow, the pigs, the alfalfa, the rabbits — in the same words he used in Chapter One. He waits until Lennie is looking away across the river. Slim's first words afterward justify him: "You hadda, George." The narrator reports the shot in one flat sentence and lets the men's reactions do the rest.[1]

Attitude word: Compassionate — the author's attitude toward this act reads as tender rather than condemnatory. A second defensible reading is Forgiving: the novella never lets anyone be blamed, and Steinbeck himself called his own way of seeing "something that happened."[4] Both are attitude words from the handout's moral list, so either earns the box; say which one you are claiming and give the evidence for it.

"Look acrost the river, Lennie"[1]

The last thing George says before he fires is a direction to look at a place that does not exist yet. The attitude is in that choice: even the killing is framed as the dream.

Mood: What the Reader Feels

The mood is built from short sentences, plain words, animals, water, and quiet before each blow. A reader might find a scene anxious, lonely, hopeful, or shocking, but those are possibilities, not the reaction the assignment requires. Choose a mood word that fits your response and explain what detail produced it. Vulnerable is one tested word from the handout; it can also describe how the dog, puppy, Lennie, and the dream are exposed to harm.[6]

Emotional Sparks: Excerpts and Reaction

The handout gives you four blank quotation lines on p. 2. Here are three brief excerpts to check, each with a chapter/search locator, a mood word, and a model reaction. Record the page from your copy beside each excerpt.

Theme & Big Ideas

What the handout asks: look "for the Big Ideas that come up in the story—these usually can be summed up in one or two words," then ask "what the main character learned; then, consider what lesson you took away that is similar to the main character but can be applied to pretty much any human" (Theme & Big Ideas.pdf, p. 1).[6]
What makes a good theme statement (Theme & Big Ideas.pdf, p. 2):[6] it must be expressed in a complete sentence; it must describe a general meaning, not specific events, actions, or characters; it must hold true for the story as a whole — beginning, middle, and end; it must avoid cliches and be stated in your own words; and the ending must match the theme you claim. "'Morality and Ethics' is not a theme statement," the handout says; "It is just pointing at a Big Idea."

Big Ideas in This Story

Every pick below is on the handout's own Big Idea chart, with the chart's category and page, so your selection can be defended from the source rather than from memory.[6]

  • Companionship — Love, p. 1. George and Lennie choose each other; everyone else is alone.
  • Friendship — Love and Circle of Life, p. 1. The same idea from a second chart.
  • Hope — Love, p. 1; War & Peace, p. 1; Judgement, p. 2. The farm is described, re-described, funded, and finally told one last time.
  • Loneliness — Suffering, p. 1. Candy, Crooks, Curley's wife, and the hired hand who earns a stake only to spend it in town again.
  • Injustice — Suffering, p. 1; Judgement, p. 2. Curley's privilege, the colour line, a woman with no name, a man who cannot protect himself.
  • Power — Survival, p. 1; Judgement, p. 2. Who can hurt whom, and how little it costs those in charge.
  • Vulnerability — Survival, p. 1. The dog, the puppy, Lennie, and the dream itself: everything tender is also breakable.
  • Fate — Love, p. 1; Judgement, p. 2; Good & Evil, p. 2. The title promises it; the novella spends more time on the people caught in it.
  • Grief and Heartbreak — Suffering, p. 1. Candy's dog, then Lennie.
  • Guilt — Suffering, p. 1; Judgement, p. 2. Candy's and George's, over the same kind of accident.
Pick two, not ten. The handout's chart exists to help you choose. A theme statement that tries to cover Companionship, Injustice, Power, and Fate at once is four themes wearing a coat; one Big Idea, tested at the beginning, the middle, and the end, is what the scoring guide rewards.

Finished Theme Statement

Template from the handout (Theme & Big Ideas.pdf, p. 2): "A central issue that reappears throughout ____ by ____ is ____. This issue is exemplified when ____ [Plot Point 1 – Beginning] ____, ____ [Plot Point 2 – Middle] ____, and ____ [Plot Point 3 – End] ____. It is at these pivotal moments the reader realizes the theme that ____ meaning that is relevant to but goes beyond the text—this should be a common human concern."

A central issue that reappears throughout Of Mice and Men by John Steinbeck is the need for companionship and a shared dream to survive hard times. This issue is exemplified when two lonely workers rehearse a promise of a little farm of their own (beginning), when an old man offers his life savings to buy into that same promise (middle), and when one friend retells the promise moments before ending the other's life (end). It is at these pivotal moments the reader realizes the theme that shared hope is what makes hardship bearable, yet dreams held by the most vulnerable people can be destroyed by a world that offers them no protection.

Why this statement works against the handout's checklist: it is one complete sentence; the final claim names no character; the same idea is tested at the beginning, the middle, and the end; it is not a cliche; it reaches past the ranch to a common human concern; and it matches the outcome — the dream dies, and the loneliness the story opened with returns in its last lines.
Step 4, the one students skip: the handout asks what the main character learned, then what lesson you took that "can be applied to pretty much any human." Here the lesson a reader takes need not be George's lesson, because George's is contested — see below. A defensible version: people who build a future together can be undone by a single accident, and the people left behind carry that story forward. That is a claim about life, not a verdict from Steinbeck.

The Ending: What the Text Actually Supports

Why this gets argued. Nearly every reader agrees on the facts: Lennie has killed Curley's wife, Curley has organised a posse, and George reaches Lennie first. Groups divide over what the shooting means, and the novella does not settle it. Steinbeck's discarded first title was Something That Happened — on the events as something nobody can be blamed for — and he described his own approach as "non-teleological thinking," a non-judgmental point of view.[4] The book's own summary states the act and its reason without judging it: George shoots Lennie "giving him a more merciful death than the one he would receive at the hands of the mob."[4] Four readings are defensible here; none of them is the only correct one.
  • Mercy. The posse is minutes away and carries rope. Slim says, "You hadda, George. I swear you hadda," and nobody argues with him. A reading that says something else has to explain why the one character who understands the situation approves of it.
  • Ending a dream that could not survive its owner. George has seen the last of it: the money is counted, the woman is dead, and Lennie has already forgotten what he did. The farm existed only for as long as the two of them could repeat it. This reading rests on structure — the last thing George does is narrate the dream again — and it does not require calling the act kind.
  • Kindness with a cost. George chooses the method and the moment, and the novella never shows Lennie's relief, because Lennie is shot from behind while looking at a place that does not exist yet. Whether that is mercy or simply the least cruel option available is precisely what the book leaves open.
  • Restraint as the point. The narrator reports the shot in one flat sentence and never returns to it. Carlson's closing question — what is wrong with those two — shows that most people in the book never learn what they saw. A group can reasonably argue that the novella delivers its judgement through structure and silence rather than argument, and that any confident moral verdict on George is the reader's, not Steinbeck's.
Make the claim, then test it. Calling George's act mercy or calling it murder can each be a defensible interpretation if the evidence and reasoning are clear. Consider the posse and Slim's approval alongside George's choice to shoot Lennie without warning, then address the strongest evidence for another reading. The course standard asks students to "cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text" (Directions & Scoring.pdf, p. 1, standard RL.1).[6]

Questions

What the handout asks: "Record any sticking points you want to get clarification from your reading group around" (Active Reading Pamphlet.pdf, p. 2).[6] A sticking point is a sentence you cannot resolve alone, so bring a quotation and a chapter number, not just an opinion. Each question below is followed by a suggested answer with its evidence — a model to argue with, not a line to copy.

1. Why does George shoot Lennie himself?

Suggested answer: because Curley has already organised a posse and would not stop, and because George believes a quick death is better than what Lennie would get from a crowd. Slim, the one man who understands both men, endorses it in a single line — "You hadda, George. I swear you hadda" — while Curley and Carlson never learn what they saw. Compare that with Carlson's closing question, "Now what the hell ya suppose is eatin' them two guys" (Ch. 6): the man who watched the whole thing still cannot read it. Ch. 6 • search: "You hadda, George"

2. Why is Curley's wife never named?

Suggested answer: the novella never gives her a personal name; "Curley's wife" identifies her through her husband's role. That choice can be read as reflecting how the ranch men reduce her to a label, and it shapes how readers first encounter her: through their warnings before hearing her at length in the barn. Her account complicates that first impression without changing the fact that she remains unnamed. Ch. 2 and Ch. 5 • search: "I'm lookin' for Curley"

3. Is the dream ever realistic?

Suggested answer: the dream becomes more concrete when Candy offers his savings, but the figures do not yet add up to the farm's price. In Chapter 3, Candy has about $350; George estimates the place at $600 and says the three of them could save enough within a month if they worked. That calculation makes the plan imaginable, not guaranteed: their wages, continued employment, and the farm's availability all matter. Ch. 3 • search: "six hundred dollars"

4. What is Candy's dog for?

Suggested answer: the dog scene can be read as a parallel to the ending: an old, vulnerable companion is killed by someone close to him, and the survivor is left with regret. Candy's line, "I ought to of shot that dog myself," comes after Carlson has shot the dog; he regrets letting a stranger do it. That later guilt may shape how a reader interprets George's choice, though the two situations are not identical. Ch. 3 • search: "I ought to of shot that dog myself"

5. Why does the story begin and end at the same pool?

Suggested answer: returning to the same river pool gives the novella a frame and makes the ending echo the opening. The setting's repeated description links the scenes, while the changed circumstances and George's final retelling of the farm dream make the return feel different. Notice which details and actions actually recur in your edition rather than assuming every movement is repeated. Ch. 1 and Ch. 6 • search: "A few miles south of Soledad"

6. Was Crooks's hope in Chapter 4 genuine?

Suggested answer: briefly, and the novella lets him say so. He asks whether he might have a small patch of garden on their place, tells them he would help with the rabbits, then immediately retreats into mockery because he has been burned before. Minutes later, threatened with being strung up, the loneliness returns. The pattern — want, joke, shame — repeats, which is why his chapter is the saddest quiet scene in the book. Ch. 4 • search: "A guy needs somebody to be near him"

7. Whose fault is the tragedy?

Suggested answer: several pressures contribute, but readers can weigh them differently. Lennie's grip causes the accident; when Curley's wife screams, he panics and tightens his hold rather than releasing it. Curley's posse makes Lennie's hiding place dangerous, while poverty and isolation shape the characters' limited choices. Steinbeck's descriptions of "something that happened" and "non-teleological thinking" may support a reading that resists a simple moral verdict[4], but they do not settle questions of responsibility. Make a claim about blame and support it with the scene. Ch. 5 • search: "Please don't do that. George'll be mad"

8. What does the title promise?

Suggested answer: it promises fate, and the novella delivers something more mixed. Burns's ploughman regrets what he could not control; George's plan is undone by an accident none of them planned. But the same story shows careless power doing deliberate damage: Curley's privilege, the colour line drawn through the harness room, a woman with no name. A strong answer says the title names the shape of the plot while the characters supply the moral pressure. Title • search: "The best laid schemes o' mice an' men"

Group tip: come with one quotation, one chapter number, and one sentence of your own. The scoring guide gives its top marks to students who "Uses directions in Yellow Pages to thoroughly complete all" the fields and cite strong, thorough textual evidence — evidence, not opinion.[6]
